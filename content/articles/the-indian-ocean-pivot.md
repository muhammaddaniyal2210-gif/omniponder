In January 2024, a container ship bound for Rotterdam turned south at the mouth of the Red Sea and added three thousand nautical miles to its voyage. It was not alone. Within weeks, the majority of traffic that would ordinarily have transited Suez was routing around the Cape of Good Hope instead, and the cost of moving a forty-foot container from Shanghai to Northern Europe had quadrupled.

The disruption was widely reported as a Red Sea story. It was not. It was an Indian Ocean story, and the distinction matters, because the vulnerability that surfaced that winter is structural rather than incidental — and it is not going away.

## The Ocean That Was Always the Centre

The habit of treating the Indian Ocean as periphery is recent and provincial. For most of recorded commercial history it was the centre of the world economy — the first genuinely multipolar trading system, and one that functioned for well over a millennium without a single dominant naval power.

The monsoon made it possible. The seasonal reversal of winds across the northern Indian Ocean gave merchants something the Atlantic would not offer for centuries: a predictable round trip. Sail west from Gujarat in one season, return in the next. That regularity produced a commercial architecture stretching from Sofala on the East African coast to Guangzhou, moving pepper, cotton, horses, porcelain, and silver through entrepôts at Aden, Hormuz, Calicut, and Malacca.

What is striking about that system, viewed from the present, is how it was governed. It largely was not. No state controlled the sea lanes. Trade operated through overlapping merchant diasporas — Gujarati, Armenian, Hadhrami, Hokkien — that enforced contracts through reputation and kinship rather than through courts or gunboats. Port cities competed to offer favourable terms because a merchant who disliked the customs regime at one harbour could simply sail to the next.

That equilibrium ended abruptly. When Vasco da Gama reached Calicut in 1498, the Portuguese introduced something the ocean had not previously contained: the armed state monopoly. The *cartaz* system required Asian vessels to purchase Portuguese passes and call at Portuguese-controlled ports. It was a protection racket enforced by naval artillery, and it established the principle that would govern the next four hundred years — that maritime commerce depends on whoever controls the narrows.

> The Indian Ocean did not become strategically important when great powers arrived. It became strategically *contested*. The trade was always there. What changed was the assumption that someone had to own the water.

## The Geography That Has Not Changed

Strip away the politics and the physical facts are unforgiving. The Indian Ocean is effectively a closed basin at its northern end, and access depends on four passages that have not moved in ten thousand years.

- **The Strait of Hormuz** — roughly 20 million barrels per day of petroleum liquids, close to a fifth of global consumption, plus a substantial share of the world's traded LNG. The navigable channel narrows to about two miles in each direction.
- **The Strait of Malacca** — comparable or greater volumes and the principal artery for energy moving to East Asia. China, Japan, and South Korea are all structurally exposed to it, and there is no economically equivalent alternative; the Lombok and Sunda routes add days and draft constraints.
- **Bab el-Mandeb** — the gateway between the Indian Ocean and the Suez corridor, and therefore the hinge on which Asia–Europe transit times turn.
- **The Cape of Good Hope** — not a chokepoint but the fallback, and the measure of what a chokepoint failure actually costs: roughly ten to fourteen additional days on the Asia–Europe run.

Around eighty percent of world merchandise trade by volume moves by sea — a share [UNCTAD's Review of Maritime Transport](https://unctad.org/topic/transport-and-trade-logistics/review-of-maritime-transport) has tracked with remarkable stability across decades of structural change — and a disproportionate share of the highest-value energy and manufactured flows converges on those four points. The [US Energy Information Administration's assessment of world oil transit chokepoints](https://www.eia.gov/international/analysis/special-topics/World_Oil_Transit_Chokepoints) has tracked this concentration for decades, and the picture has grown more rather than less concentrated as Asian demand has risen.

The consequence is a system with very little slack. There is no redundancy at Hormuz. There is no redundancy at Malacca. The Cape route exists, but it is a degradation, not a substitute — it consumes vessel capacity, fuel, and time that the global fleet does not hold in reserve.

## Rewiring the Map: What Nearshoring Actually Did

The dominant trade-policy story of the past decade has been the retreat from unconditional efficiency. Tariff regimes, export controls, industrial subsidy programmes, and the general project of reducing exposure to a single manufacturing geography have gone by various names — decoupling, de-risking, nearshoring, friendshoring — and they share an assumption that deserves examination.

The assumption is that shortening or reallocating a supply chain reduces its vulnerability. Sometimes it does. Often it relocates the vulnerability to sea.

### Diversification Lengthens the Sea Leg

When manufacturing moves from coastal China to Vietnam, India, Bangladesh, or Indonesia, it does not leave the maritime system. It moves *deeper into* the Indian Ocean basin. A Chinese export to Europe transits Malacca and then Bab el-Mandeb. An Indian export to Europe skips Malacca — but a Vietnamese or Indonesian one does not, and an Indian export to the American east coast may route through Suez or the Cape depending on conditions.

The aggregate effect of the "China plus one" strategy has been to distribute production across a wider arc of the Indian Ocean rim while leaving the exit routes precisely where they were. More origins, same doors.

There is a second-order effect that receives less attention. Fragmenting production across several countries increases the number of discrete shipments required to assemble the same finished good. A garment whose fabric is woven in one country, cut in a second, and finished in a third generates three sea legs where vertical integration generated one. Diversification of *suppliers* can therefore mean multiplication of *transits* — and each transit carries the same chokepoint exposure as the last.

### Friendshoring Is a Political Filter, Not a Physical One

Friendshoring selects trading partners by alignment. It does not select sea lanes by safety. A component manufactured in an allied state and shipped through a contested strait carries the same transit risk as one manufactured anywhere else. Political trust and physical exposure are separate variables, and policy has tended to conflate them.

### The Minerals Problem Adds a Second Leg

Electrification compounds this. Battery and grid supply chains involve ore moving from Africa, Australia, and South America to processors concentrated in Asia, and finished cells, magnets, and cathode moving back out to manufacturers. Where the hydrocarbon system had one long sea leg, the mineral system has two — and the Indian Ocean sits across both.

> Reshoring reduces dependence on a country. It does not reduce dependence on the ocean. Those are different problems, and only one of them has been seriously addressed.

## The Governance Gap

The legal architecture governing all of this is thinner than its importance suggests. The [United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea](https://www.un.org/depts/los/convention_agreements/convention_overview_convention.htm) establishes the framework — territorial seas, exclusive economic zones, and the regime of transit passage that guarantees ships and aircraft continuous and expeditious passage through straits used for international navigation.

Transit passage is the provision that keeps Hormuz and Malacca legally open. It is also, in practical terms, unenforceable by the convention itself. UNCLOS has no navy. It creates obligations and dispute mechanisms; it does not create the capability to keep a strait open against a state or non-state actor determined to close it.

For seventy years that gap was filled by an implicit arrangement in which a dominant naval power underwrote freedom of navigation as something close to a public good, and everyone else organised around the assumption that it would continue. Three developments are straining that arrangement.

**The cost-exchange ratio has inverted.** Interdiction can now be attempted with drones and anti-ship missiles costing thousands to low hundreds of thousands of dollars, while defence frequently requires interceptors an order of magnitude more expensive, launched from warships with finite magazines that cannot be reloaded at sea. A defender can win every engagement and still lose the campaign on cost.

**Naval capability has distributed.** The regional picture now includes an expanding Chinese blue-water fleet with a logistics facility at Djibouti and access arrangements across the rim, an Indian navy explicitly oriented toward the basin, and capable French, Japanese, Australian, and Gulf forces. More actors with real capability means more scenarios in which incidents escalate through miscalculation rather than intent.

**Ports have become instruments.** Long-term concessions and equity stakes in terminals from Hambantota to Gwadar to Piraeus convert commercial infrastructure into political leverage. A port is not a base, and the distinction is frequently overstated in commentary — but a state that controls berthing, bunkering, and repair across a stretch of coastline holds influence that appears in no order of battle. Access, in a long campaign, is worth more than hulls.

### Insurance as the Real Chokepoint

The mechanism by which all of this becomes economic fact is not military. It is actuarial. War risk premiums are the transmission belt between a security assessment and a shipping decision, and they can close a route far more efficiently than a blockade. In practice, underwriters decide whether a strait is open — which means a credible *threat* is often sufficient, and no shot need be fired.

## What Follows

Several conclusions sit uncomfortably with the prevailing rhetoric on trade policy.

**Resilience is a maritime problem, not only an industrial one.** A supply chain diversified across five countries that all export through the same two straits is diversified on paper and concentrated in fact. Route exposure belongs in the risk assessment alongside supplier concentration.

**Redundancy costs money and looks like waste.** Alternative routings, spare vessel capacity, larger inventories, and real stockpiles are all expensive precisely because they sit idle most of the time. Every one of them is a line item that appears indefensible until the quarter it is not.

**India's position is structurally strong and frequently underrated.** It sits astride the basin with peninsular geography, a substantial navy, and a manufacturing base that Western diversification strategies are actively courting. Whether it converts that position into durable influence is the open question of the next two decades.

**Ocean governance will lag the problem.** Legal frameworks are negotiated slowly and enforced unevenly. The gap between what UNCLOS guarantees and what any state can actually deliver at sea is where the risk lives, and nothing on the diplomatic horizon closes it.

The comfortable assumption of the past three decades was that the sea was solved — that maritime transport was a commodity input, cheap and reliable, requiring no strategic attention. That assumption held because someone was paying to make it hold, and because no one seriously tested it.

Both conditions have changed. The Indian Ocean is not becoming important; it always was. What is new is that its openness has stopped being free, and the world has not yet decided who pays.
